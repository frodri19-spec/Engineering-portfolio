About me

  

                                                                               About me 

My name is Francisco Rodriguez Gonzalez and I chose mechanical engineering because it just made sense for me. I come from a family of engineers, so I was already familiar with the field and the kind of work it involves. On top of that, I’ve always liked working on cars and bikes—taking things apart, figuring out problems, and putting them back together. Mechanical engineering felt like the right way to build on those interests and learn how to apply them on a bigger scale. What I’m excited about learning in mechanical engineering is how the principles behind machines, energy, and materials all come together to solve real-world problems. I’m excited to understand not just how things work, but how to design and improve them myself.  Currently, I am a student studying mechanical engineering, and I am in my third year here at UNC Charlotte. I am a very curious engineer and enjoy learning how things are made and how everything works together to create one final product. I would also describe myself as a hands-on individual who likes to apply the concepts I learn in class to real-life situations. One of my favorite experiences in mechanical engineering goes back to a project I did in high school. We were tasked with building an air hockey table with a robotic arm attached to it that could play hockey against one person. We had a budget of 500 dollars, which was a challenge because we had to buy everything from scratch, including the table itself, so managing costs was a big part of the project. For our design, we placed the paddle on two metal poles, allowing it to slide around using motors and a belt system. A camera mounted above the table tracked the puck’s movement and told the paddle where to go in real time. We also had to figure out what materials were available locally without going over budget. I was personally responsible for designing and building the mechanical components of the robot alongside one of my classmates. Some of my favorite memories are from troubleshooting our designs—building something, realizing it didn’t work as planned, and then going back to the drawing board to improve it. That process taught me a lot about problem-solving and persistence. Another memorable part of my journey in mechanical engineering has been the late-night study sessions with friends, trying to understand how systems work and why they behave the way they do.

 

                                                                      Force and mechanical deigns 

A concept I think is intricately woven into the fabric of design in mechanical engineering is force. Force determines everything in a product, from its size and shape to the materials it’s made from. Understanding force starts with Newton’s Laws of Motion, which describe how objects respond to applied forces and interactions with their movement. In order to understand how force affects an object, we break these complex systems into simpler parts using free-body diagrams. We also apply equations like F=ma or equations for stress to see how parts will behave under different amounts of force. Force analysis ensures that a product is functional, safe, efficient, and cost-effective. Without it, we would not be able to design mechanical systems that work reliably in the real world.

My sunglasses help keep the sun out of my eyes and fit on my head comfortably so I can wear them for long periods of time without getting tired. The authors of the design of the sunglasses is Joon H. The two small cylindrical features are the barrel hinges, which provide the arm with rotational freedom about a fixed axis, allowing the temples to fold inward and outward for compact storage. These hinges are integrated directly into the main frame (the section holding the lenses) and mechanically connect it to the arms, forming a simple pin-joint mechanism for smooth, repeatable motion.

The sunglasses lenses block sunlight, reducing glare and protecting your eyes, while the slight bend in the arms improves grip and comfort, allowing the sunglasses to stay secure and comfortable for extended wear.

The nose pads provide cushioning and grip, making the sunglasses more comfortable and stable for longer periods of wear.

 

Oakley sunglasses are designed and engineered in California, while manufacturing is split across locations. O-Matter plastic frames are injection-molded in both the U.S. and China, metal and acetate frames are mostly made in China then finished in the U.S., and lenses use Oakley’s single-pour polycarbonate process for optical precision
